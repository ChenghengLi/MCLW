\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Policy operationalisation}

We read Article~50 as a two-parameter specification problem:
\emph{what false positive rate must marked content guarantee, and at
what minimum text length?} Let $(\alpha, n_{\min})$ be this
specification. A deployer faces three choices:

\begin{enumerate}[nosep, leftmargin=*]
  \item Watermark fraction $\rho$ (controls quality cost via
    \cref{thm:quality}).
  \item State count $S$ (controls detection power via
    \cref{thm:detection}).
  \item Entropy threshold $\tau_H$ (operationally sets $\rho$; higher
    $\tau_H$ $\Rightarrow$ higher $\rho$ $\Rightarrow$ more quality cost).
\end{enumerate}

The calibration formula~\eqref{eq:calibration} reduces this to a
single constraint on $(S, \rho)$: any $(S, \rho)$ pair with
$S \geq S^\star(n_{\min}, \rho, \alpha)$ satisfies the specification.
Auditing a deployment then requires measuring three quantities
from samples -- the effective $\rho$, the key-derived $S$, and the
text-length distribution -- and checking them against the
specification. No LM access needed.

\paragraph{A minimum viable Article~50 rule.} As a concrete
illustration: set $\alpha = 10^{-6}$ and $n_{\min} = 200$. Then
\eqref{eq:calibration} with $\rho = 0.5$ gives $S^\star = 3$. A
regulator writing ``outputs shall be markable at false-positive rate
$10^{-6}$ on passages of 200 tokens or more'' would, under MCL,
translate to ``use at least 3 states at watermark fraction 0.5''.
This is a concrete, auditable, minimum-viable rule. Stronger rules
(higher $\alpha$, lower $n_{\min}$) push $S^\star$ up monotonically.

\subsection{When to use what}

For short texts ($n < 100$), detection requires large $S$ and the
calibration sits at or above the non-gated baseline. For long texts
($n > 300$), gated MCL at $S \in \{2, 3\}$ with $\rho \approx 0.5$
dominates the non-gated reference: equal detection, lower perplexity
(\cref{thm:quality}). The governance-relevant regime -- long-form
generated content subject to Article~50 -- is the one where gating
pays off.

\subsection{Limitations}

\paragraph{Tokenizer alignment.} Detection assumes the verifier uses
the same tokenizer as the generator. Adversaries who re-tokenize
via a different vocabulary can break detection without altering
surface text. This is a known limitation of token-level
watermarking schemes.

\paragraph{Key management.} If $k$ is leaked, all past and future
watermarks are exposed and replayable. MCL as presented provides no
forward security; key rotation schemes are straightforward in
principle but not evaluated here.

\paragraph{The fixed-point hypothesis.} The robustness argument for
gating rests on E4. \cref{thm:robustness} gives an unconditional
$\delta^\star$ under \emph{uniform random} modification. Real
rewriters are not uniform: they concentrate changes where the LM
allows. The low-entropy-robustness claim is that rewriters preserve
low-entropy tokens at rate $\geq q_i$ (the LM top-probability). We
test this directly; a negative result would preserve
\cref{thm:robustness} but rescind the robustness motivation for
gating specifically.

\paragraph{Quality bound is crude.} $\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathrm{KL}}] \leq
\rho \log S$ is an upper bound, not a tight characterisation. The
actual perplexity cost depends on the mass the LM concentrates in
the target partition at each gated position, which is structured
(not uniform) and model-specific.

\paragraph{Greedy decoding assumed.} MCL's determinism is compatible
with greedy decoding but not with temperature sampling, which a
deployer may require for other reasons. Temperature sampling weakens
the quality bound and does not affect detection.
